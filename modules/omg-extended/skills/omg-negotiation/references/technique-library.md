# Technique Library — 80 Named Negotiation Techniques

Scannable catalog of 80 named negotiation techniques organized by domain. Each entry is a technique you can name, recall, and deploy or counter.

**Format:** Each technique has:
- **Tell** — how to detect it (when used by counterparty against you)
- **Use** — how to deploy it (when you want to use it yourself)
- **Counter** — how to defend (your move when they use it on you)

**Canonical sources** are noted in parentheses. Full citations in `frameworks.md` and the linked research files.

---

## A. Persuasion & influence (8 techniques)

### A1. Reciprocity Trap
- **Tell:** Counterparty initiates with a small unsolicited concession or gift (waived fee, hospitality, favorable data dump). Triggers your obligation to reciprocate with a larger concession.
- **Use:** Give early, asymmetrically, and personally ("I dug into your case study"). Let them repay with leniency on a later ask.
- **Counter:** Reframe explicitly: "I appreciate the gesture — thank you. This is separate from our deal." Refuse small unsolicited favors.
- Source: Cialdini (1984, 2021).

### A2. Social Proof Anchoring
- **Tell:** "Three competitors already accepted this rate." "Everyone in the industry uses this term." Pressure via peer adoption claim.
- **Use:** Cite verified market precedent (sourced GDC talks, public deal databases, NVCA model term sheets).
- **Counter:** Demand specifics. "Which three? Names? What was their IP situation?" Unverified claims = don't engage.
- Source: Cialdini.

### A3. Scarcity Weaponization
- **Tell:** "Offer expires Friday." "Only 2 publishers left this quarter." Artificial time pressure.
- **Use:** Real, non-manipulative scarcity (genuine board meeting, fiscal year cutoff, signed LOI with another party). Verifiable.
- **Counter:** Call it. "I need 10 days due diligence — if Friday is real, walk me through what's driving it."
- Source: Cialdini.

### A4. Authority Escalation
- **Tell:** Counterparty repeatedly defers to a higher authority ("My boss won't approve") to create veto shield.
- **Use:** Establish LIMITED authority upfront: "I can commit to X. Anything above needs CEO sign-off, 48 hours."
- **Counter:** Negotiate with the decision-maker. "Is this decision yours? If not, can we include the actual authority?"
- Source: Karrass (1974), Cialdini.

### A5. Liking Cultivation
- **Tell:** Counterparty invests heavily in personal connection (find shared interests, compliments, social touch points) BEFORE asking.
- **Use:** Genuine relationship investment in cultures that require it (Korea, Vietnam, Turkey, Middle East, Latin America). NOT in task-cultures (Germany, US-direct).
- **Counter:** Recognize when liking is being used as currency. Stay rational: "I enjoy our conversations. The deal terms still need to work for both of us."
- Source: Cialdini.

### A6. Commitment & Consistency
- **Tell:** Counterparty gets you to agree to small things first ("you agree this is important?"). Then escalates to bigger asks. You stay consistent with your earlier "yes".
- **Use:** Get verbal/written commitment to PRINCIPLES early ("we agree fair revenue share is critical"). Later asks feel consistent.
- **Counter:** Recognize the ladder. Small "yeses" don't bind you to larger ones. "I agree fair revenue share is critical — and 12% is what fair looks like."
- Source: Cialdini.

### A7. Unity (Shared Identity)
- **Tell:** Counterparty emphasizes shared identity ("we're both bootstrapped founders", "we're both Asian companies fighting Western dominance"). Builds in-group loyalty.
- **Use:** Genuine. If you ARE in the same boat, say so. False unity claims backfire badly.
- **Counter:** "Yes, we're both [X] — and that's why I want to make this deal work for BOTH of us. Here's where we still need to align."
- Source: Cialdini (2016 added).

### A8. Pre-Suasion (Attention Shaping)
- **Tell:** Before the ask, counterparty primes you with a topic that biases your response ("Did you know 80% of game studios fail?" → ask comes about safety/security clauses).
- **Use:** Frame the conversation BEFORE making your ask. "I want to talk about long-term partnership..." (then propose a structure).
- **Counter:** Notice the prime. Mentally separate the framing from the substantive ask.
- Source: Cialdini, *Pre-Suasion* (2016).

## B. Behavioral economics (5 techniques)

### B1. Loss Reframing
- **Tell:** Counterparty frames concession as "what you'd lose if no deal" instead of "what you'd gain if deal."
- **Use:** Loss frame triggers ~2× emotional weight. When asking them to accept your terms, frame the alternative as their loss: "Without this milestone structure, you LOSE recoup protection."
- **Counter:** Flip the frame back. Mentally convert their loss frame to a gain frame and reset the reference point.
- Source: Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011); prospect theory.

### B2. Endowment Effect Trap
- **Tell:** Once counterparty has drafted ANY version of a clause, they overvalue it and resist changes (even objectively neutral ones). Your draft = sunk cost for them.
- **Use:** Get them to propose first on contentious terms — they'll anchor and over-defend it. You counter from a lower baseline.
- **Counter:** Don't get emotionally attached to YOUR draft. Treat it as a working doc, not a sunk cost.
- Source: Thaler (1980); Kahneman.

### B3. Decoy Effect
- **Tell:** Counterparty offers 3 options where 1 is clearly worse (the decoy). It makes the middle option look reasonable.
- **Use:** When you want them to choose Option B, present A (slightly worse), B (your target), C (much worse). B becomes the "obvious" choice.
- **Counter:** Recognize the decoy. Evaluate each option independently against your BATNA, not relative to the others.
- Source: Ariely, *Predictably Irrational* (2008).

### B4. Mental Accounting
- **Tell:** Counterparty separates total spend into mental buckets: "marketing budget", "legal budget", "advisor budget" — agrees to higher total than they would as one number.
- **Use:** Bundle several smaller asks into one mental bucket: "this is part of the partnership setup, $50K total" vs. listing 5×$10K asks.
- **Counter:** Re-aggregate. "Total ask is $50K. Let's evaluate it as one number against budget."
- Source: Thaler, *Misbehaving* (2015).

### B5. Decision Fatigue Concession
- **Tell:** Late-day, late-round, or after marathon negotiation, you concede on terms you wouldn't have at 10am.
- **Use:** Schedule final terms for morning. If fatigued, say so: "I want to think on this overnight."
- **Counter:** If THEY're fatigued, push close. If YOU're fatigued, pause.
- Source: Decision fatigue research; Kahneman (System 1/2).

## C. Game theory & commitment (5 techniques)

### C1. Focal Point Hijacking
- **Tell:** Counterparty proposes a salient solution early ("industry standard 20% royalty", "50/50 split", "calendar year boundary"). Becomes the default if unchallenged.
- **Use:** Propose your own focal point first, with a credible reason: "Recent comparable mobile games avg 14% royalty per Newzoo 2025."
- **Counter:** Counter-anchor with your own focal point in writing immediately.
- Source: Schelling, *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960).

### C2. Credible Commitment Device (Burning the Bridge)
- **Tell:** Counterparty publicly commits to a position ("we've told our board minimum 25% royalty"). Backing down costs reputation.
- **Use:** Deploy sparingly and only when you can afford to walk. Public commitment = credibility, but only if you'll honor it.
- **Counter:** If they bluff and back down once, they've burned their own credibility — note it for future rounds.
- Source: Schelling.

### C3. Tit-for-Tat with Forgiveness
- **Tell:** Counterparty matches your moves: cooperative when you cooperate, competitive when you compete. Adds occasional generosity to break deadlock.
- **Use:** Default to cooperation. Match their moves. Forgive once after defection (don't escalate to permanent hostility).
- **Counter:** Recognize this is cooperative game theory. Use it when it's working; switch to competitive only if they show sustained bad faith.
- Source: Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation* (1984).

### C4. Backward Induction
- **Tell:** Counterparty has clearly thought through the entire negotiation tree — they're not surprised by any of your moves. They've game-theoried this.
- **Use:** Before opening, write out the negotiation as a decision tree. What are their likely responses to your top 3 opening moves? What's their best counter?
- **Counter:** Don't be the unsophisticated player. Spend 30 minutes pre-game thinking about their thinking.
- Source: Dixit & Nalebuff, *Thinking Strategically* (1991).

### C5. Signaling vs. Screening
- **Tell:** Counterparty sends a costly signal that only a credible player would send (e.g., posting a bond, sharing internal financials, naming key personnel). OR they screen you with a request only a serious counterparty would grant.
- **Use:** When you have private information that benefits you, send a costly signal that demonstrates it. Cheap talk doesn't work.
- **Counter:** Match signal intensity. If they share financials, share yours (negotiated). If they post a bond, propose mutual escrow.
- Source: Spence (1973), Stiglitz; Dixit & Nalebuff.

## D. Power dynamics (5 techniques)

### D1. Power Paradox (Perspective-Blindness)
- **Tell:** High-power counterparty assumes you have the same options/information they do. Anchors on their perspective.
- **Use:** If YOU have power, deliberately take their perspective: "I know your board meets May 15 — that's why we're moving fast."
- **Counter:** If they're high-power and ignoring your constraints, make them explicit: "I have 3 approvers, not 1."
- Source: Galinsky (Columbia).

### D2. Limit Authority Gambit
- **Tell:** Counterparty offers credible authority limit ("I can commit to X, anything more needs CEO + 48 hours").
- **Use:** Deploy genuinely. Adds credibility AND gives you a delay tactic + escape hatch.
- **Counter:** Either accept the limit (if real) or escalate to the actual authority.
- Source: Karrass; Cialdini.

### D3. Walking (Credible Exit)
- **Tell:** Counterparty signals walk-away: pause, change topic, suggest "different terms next quarter".
- **Use:** Pre-rehearsed walk-away with specific trigger condition. Walk for real (3-5 days minimum) or don't walk at all.
- **Counter:** If they walk credibly, decide: chase (concede) or hold (stand firm). Don't half-chase.
- Source: Karrass; Shell.

### D4. Public Commitment Signaling
- **Tell:** "We've already announced this to our team / press / investors." Backing down costs face publicly.
- **Use:** Sparingly. Only when public commitment is credible AND walking-away is genuinely possible.
- **Counter:** Verify the public commitment exists. If yes, respect it as a signal. If no (bluff), call it.
- Source: Schelling.

### D5. BATNA Visibility
- **Tell:** Counterparty makes their BATNA visible: "We have another publisher interested at 20%." Often credible, sometimes false.
- **Use:** Make YOUR BATNA visible (only if credible): "We're funded for 18 months runway — we don't need this deal closed by Q3."
- **Counter:** Verify their BATNA. If fake, ignore. If real, work to expand your own BATNA before re-engaging.
- Source: Fisher/Ury.

## E. FBI / crisis negotiation (4 techniques)

### E1. Active Listening Stairway (5-Stage)
**Stages:** Active Listening → Empathy → Rapport → Influence → Behavioral Change.
- **Tell:** Counterparty spends 30%+ of time on listening (paraphrasing, reflecting). Influence comes much later.
- **Use:** Allocate 30% listening, 20% empathy, 30% influence, 20% close. FBI hostage success rate jumped to 90+% with this discipline.
- **Counter:** Recognize when used on you. They're building rapport deliberately. Stay anchored to your interests; don't let empathy override your walk-away.
- Source: Noesner, *Stalling for Time* (2010); FBI Behavioral Change Stairway Model.

### E2. Mirroring (Last-3-Words)
- **Tell:** Counterparty repeats your last 3 words in a questioning tone. Triggers you to elaborate or repeat (signal: where you stand).
- **Use:** Mirror their last words. They'll either entrench (signal: position-locked) or elaborate (signal: opening room). Then ask a calibrated question.
- **Counter:** When mirrored, be deliberate. Don't fill silence with concessions. Pause and respond substantively.
- Source: Voss, *Never Split the Difference* (2016).

### E3. Labeling (Name the Emotion)
- **Tell:** "It sounds like you're worried about [X]." "It seems important to you that [Y]." Names the emotion explicitly to defuse it.
- **Use:** Label proactively. Counterparty stops defending the emotion and starts defending the underlying interest.
- **Counter:** When labeled, recognize you're being defused. Don't fight it; just engage substantively.
- Source: Voss; FBI training.

### E4. Calibrated Questions (How / What)
- **Tell:** "How do we get to [X]?" "What would it take?" "What's the best way to think about this?" Open-ended, future-focused.
- **Use:** Replace yes/no questions with how/what questions. Activates problem-solving over defensive responses.
- **Counter:** Don't be lulled into endless brainstorming. Periodically reground: "Here's where we need to land."
- Source: Voss.

## F. Anchoring (5 techniques)

### F1. Extreme First Offer (65% Rule)
- **Tell:** Counterparty opens at ~65% of target. Each subsequent move smaller (20%, 10%, 5% of remaining gap).
- **Use:** Anchor at 65% of target with credible justification. Move in decreasing increments. Reach target by offer 4.
- **Counter:** Don't negotiate from their anchor. Calculate ZOPA independently. Counter-anchor with your own number.
- Source: PON Harvard; Ackerman model.

### F2. Anchoring with Data
- **Tell:** Counterparty cites specific data with sources: "Newzoo 2025 shows median publisher royalty 14%."
- **Use:** Always cite sources. Data-backed anchors stick 5-10× better than unsupported numbers.
- **Counter:** Verify the data. Cite alternative comparable sources if their citation is cherry-picked.
- Source: Bazerman; PON Harvard 2023-2025 research.

### F3. Lowball Opening
- **Tell:** Counterparty opens at 30-50% of fair value. Tests if you'll capitulate or hold.
- **Use:** Use only with strong leverage. Backfires if seen as insulting (cultures: Korean, Japanese — don't use; Turkish, Middle Eastern — expected).
- **Counter:** Don't engage with the number. "Let's set a more realistic anchor based on [data]."
- Source: Negotiation theory.

### F4. Double-Anchor (Multi-Dimensional)
- **Tell:** Counterparty anchors on 3+ dimensions simultaneously (equity + royalty + timeline + IP). You can't tell which is "the ask" and which is decoy.
- **Use:** Always anchor on multiple dimensions. Lets you trade across them in middle game.
- **Counter:** Identify which dimension matters most to them (they'll defend hardest). Trade non-priorities for priorities.
- Source: Malhotra.

### F5. Anchoring on Your Own First Offer (FAILURE MODE)
- **Tell:** YOU open at aspirational 20% royalty. They counter at 12%. You feel 16% is a "win" — when 18% might have been achievable.
- **Use:** Set anchor based on ZOPA research, not aspiration.
- **Counter:** When you catch yourself doing this, reset. Recalculate from market data.
- Source: Bazerman (anchoring bias).

## G. Information & disclosure (5 techniques)

### G1. Columbo Technique (Naive Investigator)
- **Tell:** Counterparty asks simple, "obvious" questions ("I'm new to this — how do you calculate net revenue?"). Lowers your defenses, you over-explain.
- **Use:** Genuinely useful when you actually need clarity. Apparent naïveté reveals their assumptions.
- **Counter:** Recognize when someone's "too naive" — they're probing. Stick to scripted explanations.
- Source: Columbo TV character; negotiation literature.

### G2. Strategic Information Leak
- **Tell:** Counterparty reveals specific data favorable to their position; shades or omits unfavorable data.
- **Use:** Reveal what supports your position. Don't volunteer constraints/weaknesses.
- **Counter:** Ask directly for full picture. Use due diligence as a flushing tool: "Send me your last 4 quarters of P&L."
- Source: General negotiation theory.

### G3. Silence as Tactic
- **Tell:** After your offer, counterparty stays silent for 10-20 seconds. You feel pressure to fill the silence with concessions.
- **Use:** Make a bold offer, then shut up. Let THEM break silence. Count internally to 15 if needed.
- **Counter:** Recognize the silence and don't break it. Match their silence. Eventually one of you speaks substantively, not desperately.
- Source: Voss; FBI training.

### G4. Documentation Trail
- **Tell:** Counterparty summarizes every call in email immediately after. Creates paper trail for accountability.
- **Use:** Send written recaps after every meaningful exchange. Locks in agreements; surfaces misunderstandings; creates evidence.
- **Counter:** Always read their recap carefully. Correct mischaracterizations within 24 hours or you're deemed to have agreed.
- Source: General contract practice.

### G5. Hypothetical Sequencing
- **Tell:** "IF we did X, would you then do Y?" Explores positions without committing.
- **Use:** Probe limits without revealing your own. "Hypothetically, if equity were 8%, would Pool A milestones loosen?"
- **Counter:** Don't commit to hypotheticals. "Hypothetically, anything is possible. What's the actual proposal?"
- Source: Malhotra.

## H. Concession discipline (6 techniques)

### H1. Ackerman Model (Decreasing Increments)
- **Structure:** Offer 1: 65% of target. Offer 2: +20% of gap (=85%). Offer 3: +10% of gap (=95%). Offer 4: +5% of gap (=100%).
- **Tell:** Counterparty's increments shrink predictably.
- **Use:** Pair with empathy: "I'm hitting internal constraints; this is as far as I can push."
- **Counter:** If their 4th offer is 10% short of your minimum, ZOPA failed. Walk or hold firm.
- Source: Voss; CIA-derived.

### H2. Karrass Decreasing-Step Rule
- **Tell:** Counterparty's concessions get smaller each round (signals proximity to limit).
- **Use:** Document your concession sequence in writing before negotiation. Stick to the discipline.
- **Counter:** Watch for the inverse — if their concessions are GROWING, they have headroom you haven't tapped.
- Source: Karrass, *Give and Take* (1974).

### H3. Conditional Concession (No Freebies)
- **Tell:** Counterparty offers Term A only IF you give them Term B. Trades, never concedes.
- **Use:** Never concede unilaterally. "If we move on royalty, you need to extend term to 5 years."
- **Counter:** Always trade. If they concede unilaterally, ask: what's behind it?
- Source: Karrass; Voss.

### H4. Package Deals (Multi-Dimensional Trades)
- **Tell:** Counterparty proposes bundles: "We'll do A and B if you do C and D."
- **Use:** Pre-build 3-4 bundle scenarios before negotiation. Each bundle = different path to mutual gain. Let them choose.
- **Counter:** Engage with the WHOLE bundle, don't pick at one element. "I'm willing on A+B if C drops by 20%."
- Source: Malhotra.

### H5. Nibble Defense
- **Tell:** Late-stage small new asks ("just one more thing — non-disparagement clause").
- **Use:** Pre-commit to round discipline. "Round 8 is final. New asks need new concessions from us."
- **Counter:** Refuse free nibbles. Force trade. "Non-disparagement is fine — and we add equivalent obligation on you."
- Source: Dawson, *Secrets of Power Negotiating* (2010).

### H6. Red Herring Information (False Concession)
- **Tell:** Counterparty surfaces a "concession" on something they were never going to fight for. You feel obligated to reciprocate.
- **Use:** Surface an unimportant point as your "concession" to build reciprocity bank.
- **Counter:** Recognize fake concessions. "Thanks for moving on X — let's also address Y." Don't trade real for fake.
- Source: General negotiation tactics.

## I. Procurement & vendor (4 techniques)

### I1. Kraljic Matrix Positioning
- **Quadrants:** (Strategic, Leverage, Bottleneck, Non-Critical) based on (supply risk, profit impact).
- **Tell:** Counterparty positions you in the wrong quadrant ("you're just one of many suppliers" when you're actually strategic).
- **Use:** Identify your real position. If strategic, build relationship + show switching costs. If leverage, reinforce competition.
- **Counter:** If they claim you're leverage when you're strategic, make switching costs visible: "Our integration is 6 months; moving = $200K + 4 months."
- Source: Kraljic, HBR (1983).

### I2. Reverse Auction
- **Tell:** Counterparty pits multiple suppliers against each other in real-time bidding.
- **Use:** When YOU're the buyer with multiple credible vendors, force visible competition.
- **Counter:** When you're the supplier, refuse the reverse auction format. "I provide premium service; that's not commodity bidding."
- Source: Procurement literature.

### I3. Should-Cost Analysis
- **Tell:** Counterparty claims to know "what it should cost" — probably from cost modeling.
- **Use:** Build your own should-cost analysis. Identify margin layers. Negotiate the margin you're willing to give them.
- **Counter:** Defend your margin with value: "Here's what we provide that justifies the margin." Show, don't just claim.
- Source: Procurement / supply chain.

### I4. Total Cost of Ownership (TCO)
- **Tell:** Counterparty focuses only on price. Ignores support, training, integration, switching costs.
- **Use:** Reframe to TCO. "Cheaper licensing + 6-month integration = $X total. Our offer = $Y total. We're cheaper at TCO."
- **Counter:** Demand TCO transparency. "What's the total over 3 years? Including these support costs?"
- Source: Procurement.

## J. Multi-party & coalition (4 techniques)

### J1. Log-Rolling
- **Tell:** Different parties care about different issues. Trades become possible across dimensions ("you care about Issue A, I care about Issue B — let's trade").
- **Use:** Identify each party's top 1-2 priorities. Trade what matters most to them for what matters most to you.
- **Counter:** Recognize when a party is logrolling against you. Don't accept trades that look favorable but actually move you off your priorities.
- Source: Multi-party negotiation theory.

### J2. Coalition Formation
- **Tell:** In 3+ party negotiations, two parties align against a third.
- **Use:** Identify your natural ally early. Lock in alignment before main negotiation. "We share the same concern — let's agree on baseline."
- **Counter:** If you're being coalition-flanked, peel one party off. "I notice we agree on X. Why are we on different sides on Y?"
- Source: Multi-party theory.

### J3. Divide and Conquer
- **Tell:** Counterparty negotiates with each member of your team separately, getting different commitments.
- **Use:** Negotiate 1:1 with their parties before group meeting (Japanese nemawashi). Build consensus 1-by-1.
- **Counter:** Single point of contact. "All commitments come through me." Internal team alignment before each session.
- Source: Multi-party theory.

### J4. Side Payments
- **Tell:** One party gets a non-deal benefit (cash, marketing, tech) to shift their position.
- **Use:** Offer a side benefit to a party that's blocking you, separate from main deal terms.
- **Counter:** Detect side payments — they shift incentives invisibly. Demand transparency: "Are there other arrangements I should know about?"
- Source: Multi-party theory.

## K. Cultural patterns (12 techniques)

### K1. Nemawashi (Japanese Pre-Negotiation Consensus)
- **Tell:** Japanese counterparty doesn't decide in the room. Decisions emerge from prior 1:1 conversations.
- **Use:** Have individual conversations BEFORE the formal meeting. Address concerns 1:1.
- **Counter:** Don't accept "we'll discuss internally" as a black box. Push for visibility into stakeholders.
- Source: Japanese business culture.

### K2. Honne vs. Tatemae
- **Tell:** Japanese counterparty says "yes" (tatemae/public face) but means "no" (honne/true feeling). Public dissent is shameful.
- **Use:** Create space for private feedback. "Anything that concerns you off-record?"
- **Counter:** Don't assume "yes" is agreement. Probe gently for reservations.
- Source: Japanese culture.

### K3. Nunchi (Korean Situational Awareness)
- **Tell:** Korean counterparty rapidly reads room dynamics, hierarchy, unspoken cues. Adjusts behavior.
- **Use:** Be aware they're reading you. Control nonverbals. Use deliberate pauses (signals thinking).
- **Counter:** Be transparent about your read. "I sense some concern from [person] — let's address it."
- Source: Korean culture; Hong, *The Power of Nunchi* (2019).

### K4. Jeong & Kibun (Korean Emotional Bond + Pride)
- **Tell:** Korean culture builds emotional connection (jeong) and protects standing (kibun). Public criticism damages both.
- **Use:** Build jeong via shared meals, small gestures. Disagree 1:1, never in public. Protect kibun.
- **Counter:** If they're using jeong with you, enjoy but don't let it cloud judgment.
- Source: Korean culture.

### K5. Guanxi (Chinese Relationship Capital)
- **Tell:** Chinese counterparty frames deal as start of long-term obligation banking, not isolated transaction.
- **Use:** Frame as relationship: "This is project 1. If it succeeds, project 2 follows."
- **Counter:** Don't let guanxi pressure override deal terms. "We have guanxi AND we have terms."
- Source: Chinese culture; PON Harvard.

### K6. Mianzi (Face Preservation)
- **Tell:** Public contradiction or pointing out errors in front of others causes face-loss.
- **Use:** Disagree privately. Validate publicly: "Your approach is sound; I want to add this angle."
- **Counter:** If they're protecting their face at your expense ("I said yes privately, but publicly I need to claim a win"), accommodate face-saving while protecting substance.
- Source: Chinese culture.

### K7. Indirect "No" Masquerade (Vietnamese)
- **Tell:** Vietnamese rarely say no directly. Instead: "Maybe", "I will think", "That might be difficult".
- **Use:** Probe these phrases. "Help me understand what makes it difficult."
- **Counter:** Confirm in writing. "Just to confirm, we agreed to X, Y, Z?"
- Source: Vietnamese culture.

### K8. Honor & Bargaining Ritual (Turkish)
- **Tell:** Turkish counterparty haggles extensively. Opening at 65% is normal. Not bargaining = disrespect.
- **Use:** Match haggling intensity. Don't soft-pedal. Each move should feel earned.
- **Counter:** Establish absolute walk-away first. Let them bargain around it without moving your core.
- Source: Turkish culture.

### K9. Emotional Transparency (Turkish)
- **Tell:** Turkish counterparty values directness and emotion (passion, even mild anger) as conviction signals.
- **Use:** Be authentic. Don't be cold.
- **Counter:** If emotion is being used to manipulate, acknowledge but stay rational.
- Source: Turkish culture.

### K10. Precision First (German)
- **Tell:** German counterparty wants every detail clear, in writing, before agreement.
- **Use:** Draft detailed term sheets early. Be precise.
- **Counter:** Don't try to hide flexibility in ambiguity. Be transparent about flex needs.
- Source: German culture.

### K11. Facts Over Emotions (German)
- **Tell:** German counterparty argues from data, dismisses emotional appeals.
- **Use:** Lead with research. "Three comparable deals show 18-22% royalty."
- **Counter:** If they cite questionable data, challenge sourcing.
- Source: German culture.

### K12. Avoid Deadline Pressure Threats (Turkish)
- **Tell:** Turkish counterparty resents tight artificial deadlines. Will escalate or threaten walk.
- **Use:** Real deadlines (board approval) are fine. Artificial deadlines backfire.
- **Counter:** If they impose tight deadlines on you, push back calmly: "Let's find a path that respects both schedules."
- Source: Turkish culture.

## L. Modern behavioral research (3 techniques)

### L1. Emotion Over Data (Sharot)
- **Tell:** Counterparty dismisses your data when it conflicts with how they feel about the deal.
- **Use:** Lead with emotion/relationship, then support with data. Two systems aligned.
- **Counter:** If they lead with emotion, address emotion first. "Help me understand what's making this uncomfortable" before metrics.
- Source: Sharot, *The Influential Mind* (2017).

### L2. Seven Core Elements of Influence (Sharot)
**Elements:** prior beliefs, emotions, incentives, sense of agency, curiosity, state of mind, knowledge of others' actions.
- **Tell:** Generic persuasion doesn't move counterparty. They have specific frame.
- **Use:** Probe their worldview first. "What's important to you in a publisher partnership?" Frame your offer within it.
- **Counter:** Recognize when being framed. Don't accept the frame without evaluation.
- Source: Sharot.

### L3. Inner Board of Directors (Fox)
- **Components:** Dreamer (vision), Thinker (logic), Lover (relationship), Warrior (assertiveness).
- **Tell:** External negotiation fails when internal negotiators (yours or theirs) aren't aligned.
- **Use:** Pre-negotiation: which "inner director" is dominant? If "Lover" wants relationship and "Warrior" wants results, resolve internally first.
- **Counter:** If counterparty seems conflicted, call it. "I sense hesitation — what needs clarifying internally?"
- Source: Fox, *Winning From Within* (2013).

## M. M&A specific (3 techniques)

### M1. Earn-Out as Negotiation Reset
- **Tell:** Buyer/seller valuations diverge. Earn-out bridges: cash upfront + contingent additional.
- **Use:** Propose when above their valuation but confident in growth. "$8M cash + up to $2M if 500K DAU at Year 2."
- **Counter:** Demand auditable KPIs. Avoid ambiguity. Insist on: exact metric, measurement method, payment timing, claw-back caps.
- Source: M&A practice.

### M2. Reps & Warranties (Risk Allocation)
- **Tell:** Buyer wants broad reps; seller wants narrow.
- **Use:** As seller: narrow to what you actually know. As buyer: broaden via due diligence.
- **Counter:** Reps must be objective, not aspirational. Avoid "game will succeed".
- Source: M&A practice.

### M3. Material Adverse Change (MAC) Clauses
- **Tell:** Buyer adds MAC clause allowing walk-away if "material adverse change" occurs between signing and closing.
- **Use:** As seller, define MAC narrowly: specific events, specific magnitudes, with carve-outs for general market.
- **Counter:** As buyer, broaden MAC; as seller, narrow it. Both sides should agree on definition.
- Source: M&A practice.

## N. Failure modes (5 techniques to AVOID)

### N1. Splitting the Difference Trap
- **Anti-Pattern:** Both sides anchor far from real positions, then "compromise" at midpoint. Both leave worse than alternative trades.
- **Avoid:** Refuse splits. Trade across dimensions instead.
- Source: Voss.

### N2. Reciprocity Exploitation (One-Way)
- **Anti-Pattern:** You concede repeatedly; they don't reciprocate. By round 3, you've normalized one-way movement.
- **Avoid:** After EACH concession, ask: "What do you move on in return?"
- Source: Cialdini (defensive read).

### N3. Anchoring on Your Own First Offer
- **Anti-Pattern:** You open at aspiration. They counter credibly. You feel midpoint is a "win" when better was achievable.
- **Avoid:** Anchor based on ZOPA research, not aspiration.
- Source: Bazerman.

### N4. Decision Fatigue Concession
- **Anti-Pattern:** Late-day, late-round, you concede on terms you wouldn't have at 10am.
- **Avoid:** Schedule final decisions for morning. "I want to think on this overnight."
- Source: Decision research.

### N5. Escalation of Commitment (Sunk Cost)
- **Anti-Pattern:** 6 months into a stuck negotiation, you sign a bad deal because "we've come too far to walk."
- **Avoid:** Recalculate BATNA every 2 rounds. If ZOPA disappears, walk regardless of sunk cost.
- Source: Staw (1976) escalation research.

## O. Advanced & async (4 techniques)

### O1. 24-Hour Delay Rule (Email Tempo)
- **Tell:** Email negotiations escalate fast. Counterparty sends aggressive message; you respond same-day matching tone.
- **Use:** For contentious emails: draft response, sleep on it, send next day.
- **Counter:** If they push for same-day, don't capitulate. "I need 24 hours to consider."
- Source: Best practice.

### O2. Voice Call Escalation
- **Tell:** After 3 email exchanges with no progress, the issue is too nuanced for email.
- **Use:** Move to call. "I think we're circling — let's talk Wednesday at 3pm."
- **Counter:** If they avoid calls (only emailing), push for voice. "I need to hear your thinking."
- Source: Async negotiation literature.

### O3. Pre-Mortem (Klein 2007)
- **Tell:** Before signing, convene both teams. "Imagine the deal failed in 18 months — list every reason."
- **Use:** Reveals hidden assumptions before signing. Fix problems early.
- **Counter:** If they resist pre-mortem ("negative thinking"), reframe positively: "Let's stress-test."
- Source: Klein, *Sources of Power* (1998); pre-mortem methodology.

### O4. Cooperation/Competition Switch (Galinsky/Schweitzer)
- **Tell:** Humans default to one mode (cooperation or competition); switch based on signals.
- **Use:** Mirror their stance. Collaborative when they are; competitive when they are.
- **Counter:** Recognize switches. "I notice the tone shifted — did something change?"
- Source: Galinsky & Schweitzer, *Friend and Foe* (2015).

## P. Tactical roles & emotion (3 techniques)

### P1. Good-Cop / Bad-Cop
- **Tell:** Multi-person team. One warm, one harsh. Alternate.
- **Use:** Sparingly — Korea/Japan dislike this. Works in adversarial cultures.
- **Counter:** Stay consistent regardless of tone. Respond to substance only.
- Source: General tactics.

### P2. Tactical Anger / Controlled Venting
- **Tell:** Counterparty displays frustration to signal seriousness (not loss of control).
- **Use:** Strategic display of frustration on a specific issue. Then move past it. Use sparingly.
- **Counter:** Don't escalate. "I hear that this matters to you. Let me address the substance."
- Source: Voss, Goffman face-work.

### P3. Goffman Face-Work
- **Tell:** Counterparty manages impressions of self — "saving face" mechanics in public vs private.
- **Use:** Always give public face; reserve hard pushback for private.
- **Counter:** If they're trying to win publicly while losing privately, accommodate face but enforce substance.
- Source: Goffman, *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1956).

## Q. Trust building (4 techniques)

### Q1. Personal Liability (Skin in Game)
- **Tell:** "I personally guarantee this number." Signals confidence and risk-taking.
- **Use:** Use sparingly. Only when you can actually back it up.
- **Counter:** Test the guarantee. Make it specific in writing. If they back out, the relationship is dead.
- Source: Trust-building literature.

### Q2. Third-Party Validation
- **Tell:** "Our lawyer / banker / advisor reviewed; terms are fair."
- **Use:** Cite specific independent expert with credibility.
- **Counter:** Verify the third party exists. Cite your own expert if their citation is weak.
- Source: Authority and social proof.

### Q3. Reputational Stake
- **Tell:** "We've worked with X publishers — our reputation is on the line if we miss KPIs."
- **Use:** Genuine reputational stakes build trust.
- **Counter:** Verify reputation claims. If they're real, leverage them: "Given your reputation, this should be no issue for you."
- Source: Trust-building.

### Q4. Bridging Proposal
- **Tell:** "Let's try X for 12 months, then reassess." Reduces risk for both via trial period.
- **Use:** When both sides are uncertain, trial structure beats upfront commitment.
- **Counter:** Insist on clear evaluation criteria for the trial. Vague trials become permanent without explicit decisions.
- Source: Malhotra.

## R. Closing tactics (4 techniques)

### R1. Highest and Best Offer (HBO)
- **Tell:** "Last and final offer, take it or leave it."
- **Use:** Only when you can credibly walk after.
- **Counter:** "Is this truly final, or is there room?" Test if real.
- Source: Procurement.

### R2. Manufactured Urgency (CAUTION)
- **Tell:** Artificial deadline ("ends Friday") to force decision.
- **Use:** Only deploy if real (board meeting, fiscal year). Backfires badly if detected as fake.
- **Counter:** Demand the source of urgency. "What happens Friday that doesn't happen Monday?"
- Source: General tactics; Cialdini scarcity.

### R3. Option Expansion
- **Tell:** When stuck on Term A, introduce Term B option: "If royalty is fixed, can payment timing be variable?"
- **Use:** Always have 1-2 alternative dimensions ready when primary stalls.
- **Counter:** Engage with the new option, but don't lose track of the original. Sometimes Option B is a deflection.
- Source: Malhotra, problem-solving framework.

### R4. Reframing (Perspective Shift)
- **Tell:** Counterparty presents your constraint as opportunity: "You need quick revenue — earnouts align our interests."
- **Use:** When stuck, reframe constraints as enabling. Both sides win narratives.
- **Counter:** Verify the reframe is legitimate. Sometimes "alignment" rhetoric hides bad terms.
- Source: Voss; Malhotra.
