# Frameworks — 5 Foundational Negotiation Theories

Deep dive on the five canonical negotiation frameworks. These are the theoretical anchors for every other reference file in this skill. Read this once; refer back when you need the WHY behind a tactic.

---

## 1. Principled Negotiation (Fisher / Ury / Patton)

**Source:** *Getting to Yes* (1981, 3rd ed 2011), Harvard Negotiation Project.

### Core thesis
Separate people from the problem. Focus on interests, not positions. Generate options for mutual gain. Insist on objective criteria. Build the "expanded pie" mentality where both parties leave richer than they entered.

### Four pillars

**1. Separate the people from the problem.**
The negotiation is about the deal, not about your relationship with the counterparty. Get angry, but at the issue, not the person. Build personal rapport even when fighting hard on terms.

*Application:* When Alfa pushed back hard on 22 clauses, the relationship doesn't degrade — only the substantive disagreement remains. After signing, you'll need to work together for years.

**2. Focus on interests, not positions.**
A position is what they say they want ("40% equity"). An interest is the underlying need ("control over milestone decisions"). Negotiate the interest, not the position.

*Application:* When BagelCode demanded "all IP", their position is "all IP" but their interest is "no competing product blocks our market". You can solve the interest with non-compete + first-refusal without surrendering all IP.

**3. Generate options for mutual gain.**
Before deciding, brainstorm options. Multi-dimensional trades unlock value that single-dimension haggling cannot. Royalty + milestones + IP + timeline + territory + approval rights = 6 dimensions to trade across.

*Application:* Instead of fighting on royalty alone, propose: "12% royalty + quarterly bonus tied to ARPU + co-marketing in 3 regions + IP reversion at year 5 + approval on 2 sequels." Both sides find dimensions to claim wins.

**4. Insist on objective criteria.**
When stuck, propose external criteria as the decider: industry benchmarks, comparable deals, third-party valuations, NVCA model term sheets. Frame disagreement as "disagreement with the data," not with you.

*Application:* "Newzoo 2025 data shows median publisher royalty is 14%. We can go up or down from that, but let's anchor on the data."

### Critical concept: BATNA

**Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement.** The single most important pre-negotiation analysis. Your BATNA is what you do if no deal happens. It defines your reservation price (walk-away point).

- **Strong BATNA** (multiple alternatives, time on your side, profitable without deal) → high negotiating power
- **Weak BATNA** (need this deal, no alternatives, time pressure) → low negotiating power
- **Pre-negotiation work:** strengthen BATNA before entering negotiation. Multiple alternatives = leverage.

### When this framework applies
- Most commercial negotiations with rational counterparties
- Multi-party agreements with shared long-term interests
- Negotiations where relationship matters post-deal

### When this framework fails
- Genuinely zero-sum situations (lottery split, fixed-budget allocations)
- Counterparty is irrational or actively bad-faith
- Cultural contexts where directness is offensive (Korea, Japan, Vietnam — apply with cultural overlay)

## 2. Tactical Empathy (Voss)

**Source:** *Never Split the Difference* (2016), Chris Voss (former FBI hostage chief).

### Core thesis
Empathy is a negotiating tool, not warmth. Mirror their emotions, label them, and ask calibrated questions to defuse emotional escalation and unlock flexibility. Voss treats every negotiation like a hostage negotiation — high emotional stakes, irrationality everywhere, the goal is behavioral change.

### Five core moves

**1. Mirroring.**
Repeat the last 3 words of counterparty's statement in a questioning tone. They'll either repeat themselves (entrenched) or elaborate (opening up). Cheap, easy, devastating.

*Example:* Them: "We need approval rights on all marketing." You: "Approval rights on all marketing?" (pause). They'll either re-cement or reveal what they actually mean.

**2. Labeling.**
Explicitly name the emotion you observe. "It sounds like you're concerned we'll deprioritize this title after Series B." Creates psychological distance — they stop defending the emotion and start defending the interest.

*Example:* BagelCode said "Korean publishers get screwed by foreign studios." Label: "You're concerned we'll ghost after funding dries up." They can agree without losing face. Then you propose: monthly milestone reviews.

**3. Calibrated questions ("How" / "What", future-focused).**
Replace yes/no questions with how/what. Activates problem-solving instead of defense.

*Examples:*
- Bad: "Will you accept 15%?"
- Good: "What would it take to get to 15%?"
- Better: "How do we structure this so you don't feel squeezed below 20% but we can live with that?"

**4. "That's right" vs. "You're right".**
"You're right" = lip service. "That's right" = behavioral shift. After a reframing, ask "Is that right?" not "Do you agree?". If "That's right", move on. If "You're right" or just nodding, they haven't internalized — circle back.

**5. Accusation audit (proactive objection inoculation).**
Before counterparty raises their objection, you raise it first and refute it. "I know you think we're going to milk soft-launch and ghost. We're not. Here's why: [1, 2, 3]." Removes the sting and signals confidence.

### Critical concept: The "no"-ladder
Voss inverts conventional wisdom: "no" is the start of negotiation, not the end. People defend "no" stronger than "yes". Aim for "no"-oriented questions: "Is now a bad time to talk?" gets them to say "no" (= meaning yes, you can talk) and feel in control.

### When this framework applies
- High-emotion negotiations
- Counterparty is anxious, defensive, or position-locked
- Cross-cultural with face-saving sensitivity (Korea, Japan, China)
- Multi-round with relationship stakes

### When this framework fails
- Pure-data negotiations with rational counterparties (German engineering, US tech)
- Quick transactional deals (no time for tactical empathy)
- Counterparty is using Voss against you (recognize and respond substantively, don't get emotionally seduced)

## 3. Concession Discipline & Leverage Analysis (Karrass / Shell)

**Sources:** Karrass, *Give and Take* (1974); Shell, *Bargaining for Advantage* (2nd ed 2006).

### Core thesis
Concessions are signals. Decreasing concessions signal "near my limit"; increasing concessions signal weakness. Concession discipline is the highest-ROI negotiation skill. Pair with explicit leverage analysis (positive / negative / normative axes).

### Karrass: Decreasing-step rule

Every concession should be smaller than the last:
- Round 1: 5-point move
- Round 2: 2-point move + small bundle
- Round 3: 0.5-point tweak

If you offer constant or growing concessions, counterparty assumes infinite headroom. If you offer decreasing concessions, counterparty assumes you're at limit.

**Document your concession sequence in writing BEFORE negotiation.** Discipline beats intuition under pressure.

### Karrass: Higher Authority

When cornered, claim "I need to check with my [board / partner / lawyer]". Breaks deadlock; gives delay tactic; creates credible excuse to walk back.

If counterparty does this to YOU: "Let's loop them in via email so we're all aligned." Real authorities accept; fake ones backtrack.

### Shell: Three-axis leverage analysis

Three sources of negotiating power:

| Axis | Definition | Examples (gaming partnership) |
|------|------------|-------------------------------|
| **Positive** | What you can do FOR them | Funding, audience, team, tech, distribution |
| **Negative** | What you can do TO them | Walk away, fund competitor, publicize bad terms, legal action |
| **Normative** | What 3rd parties / industry / law expect | Publisher rates, IP norms, VC term sheet standards |

**Pre-negotiation:** rank top 3 positive, top 2 negative, top 2 normative. Decide which to lead with for each counterparty.

*Application — BagelCode:*
- Positive: proven mobile dev team
- Negative: self-fund or alternative publisher
- Normative: IP ownership in games skews 70% to developer (GDC data)
- Strategy: Lead with normative ("IP assignment is unusual"), then positive (prove self-funding capability)

### Shell: Five negotiation styles
Negotiators have natural styles. Diagnose yours and counterparty's:
- **Compete:** wants to win, comfortable with confrontation
- **Accommodate:** prioritizes relationship over outcome
- **Avoid:** delays, sidesteps, refuses engagement
- **Compromise:** quick to split-the-difference
- **Collaborate:** seeks expanded pie

Best style: Collaborate (when possible). Worst styles to be locked into: Accommodate (alone) or Avoid (alone). The best negotiators are flexible across styles.

### When this framework applies
- ALL multi-round negotiations (concession discipline is universal)
- High-stakes deals with leverage on both sides
- Multi-party negotiations (different leverage with each party)

### When it fails
- Single-shot negotiations with no concession sequence (just take-it-or-leave-it)
- Cultures where bargaining itself is structured (Turkish, Middle Eastern — adapt to expected ritual)

## 4. Cognitive Bias Mitigation (Bazerman / Neale / Malhotra)

**Sources:** Bazerman & Neale, *Negotiating Rationally* (1992); Malhotra & Bazerman, *Negotiation Genius* (2007).

### Core thesis
Negotiators are systematically irrational — anchoring bias, sunk-cost fallacy, escalation of commitment, fixed-pie assumption, overconfidence, framing effects. Mitigation requires written plans, third-party review, and decision analysis BEFORE emotional investment.

### The 6 biases that destroy negotiations

**1. Anchoring bias.**
The first number disproportionately influences the outcome, even when both parties know it's extreme. If they anchor at 50% equity, your counter at 10% leaves midpoint at 30% — instead of 20% if you anchored first.

*Mitigation:* Anchor first in writing. Aggressive but defensible (cite benchmarks).

**2. Fixed-pie assumption.**
Both parties assume zero-sum: "if they get more, I get less". False. Multi-dimensional trades expand the pie.

*Mitigation:* In middle game, propose: "If royalty drops to 12%, can we add quarterly milestone bonus?" Trade across dimensions.

**3. Sunk-cost fallacy / Escalation of commitment.**
You've negotiated 5 rounds. You feel obligated to accept terms you wouldn't have on day 1.

*Mitigation:* Recalculate BATNA every 2 rounds. Pretend it's day 1. Would you accept these terms now? If not, walk.

**4. Overconfidence.**
You overestimate counterparty's understanding of your position. They demand things that make no sense given what you've told them.

*Mitigation:* Send written term sheets, email recaps. Misunderstanding at round 5 is expensive; at round 1 is cheap.

**5. Framing effects.**
"You'll lose $1M" hits 2× harder than "You'll gain $1M". Same number, different emotional weight.

*Mitigation:* Frame your asks as gains for them; frame their refusals as losses for them. Standard prospect-theory leverage.

**6. Reactive devaluation.**
Whatever the counterparty proposes is automatically discounted ("if they're proposing it, it must benefit them more"). This kills good deals.

*Mitigation:* Anchor proposals in objective criteria so they don't seem self-serving. Or have a third party propose the compromise.

### Malhotra: BATNA expansion

Don't accept your starting BATNA. Spend 1-2 weeks BEFORE negotiation strengthening it:
- Approach 2-3 alternative counterparties (even just exploratory)
- Identify "do nothing" outcomes more carefully
- Build coalitions that strengthen your position
- Improve your standalone capability (capital, talent, customers)

A negotiator with a stronger BATNA than 2 weeks ago has more leverage even if nothing else changed.

### Malhotra: Pie expansion moves
- **Logrolling:** trade across issues both parties care about differently
- **Contingency contracts:** "If X happens, you pay Y" — reduces uncertainty risk
- **Compromise across multiple dimensions:** never split a single dimension; trade across two

### When this framework applies
- ALL negotiations (biases are universal)
- Especially: multi-round, complex (multi-dimensional), high-stakes

### When it fails
- Quick simple deals (no time for written analysis)
- Counterparty is genuinely irrational (use Voss instead)

## 5. Cultural Adaptation (Meyer)

**Source:** *The Culture Map* (2014), Erin Meyer.

### Core thesis
Trust is built differently across cultures. "Universal" negotiation frameworks fail when cross-cultural without adaptation. Meyer's 8 cultural dimensions help diagnose differences.

### The 8 dimensions (negotiation-relevant)

**1. Communicating: Low-context vs. high-context.**
- Low-context (US, Germany, Australia): explicit, direct, written-friendly
- High-context (Japan, Korea, China): implicit, indirect, requires reading between lines

**2. Evaluating: Direct negative feedback vs. indirect.**
- Direct (Israel, Russia, Germany, Netherlands): blunt criticism in public
- Indirect (Japan, Korea, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia): face-saving, private feedback only

**3. Persuading: Principles-first vs. applications-first.**
- Principles-first (France, Germany): theory then example
- Applications-first (US, UK): example then theory

**4. Leading: Egalitarian vs. hierarchical.**
- Egalitarian (Denmark, Netherlands, Israel): challenge boss directly
- Hierarchical (Korea, Japan, China, Russia): defer to seniority always

**5. Deciding: Consensual vs. top-down.**
- Consensual (Japan, Sweden, Germany): everyone aligned before decision
- Top-down (China, Russia, Italy, India): boss decides, others execute

**6. Trusting: Task-based vs. relationship-based.**
- Task-based (US, Germany, UK, Switzerland): trust = competence and results
- Relationship-based (China, Brazil, Saudi Arabia, Vietnam): trust = personal bond over time

**7. Disagreeing: Confrontational vs. avoids confrontation.**
- Confrontational (Israel, France, Russia): debate openly, no offense
- Avoids confrontation (Japan, Indonesia, Thailand): harmony at all costs

**8. Scheduling: Linear-time vs. flexible-time.**
- Linear-time (Germany, Switzerland, Japan): deadlines are sacred
- Flexible-time (India, Saudi Arabia, Brazil): time bends to relationship

### Application matrix

For your active counterparties:

| Counterparty | Trust | Disagreeing | Deciding | Communicating |
|--------------|-------|-------------|----------|---------------|
| Bagelcode (Korea) | Mixed | Avoids confrontation | Top-down (consensus signaling) | High-context |
| JM Game (HK) | Mixed | Mixed | Top-down | Mixed |
| PlayableLab (VN) | Relationship | Avoids confrontation | Consensus | High-context |
| Alfa (Turkey) | Relationship | Mixed-confrontational | Top-down | Mixed |

**Practical impact:**
- With Korea: indirect disagreement, never criticize publicly, signal "consensus emerging" even when you decided
- With Vietnam: long timelines, multiple stakeholders, consensus required, written confirmation
- With Turkey: comfortable haggling, direct feedback, decisions can be top-down (single signatory possible)
- With HK: mix of all above, lean into precision and arbitration-comfortable language

### When this framework applies
- ALL cross-cultural negotiations
- Especially: deals lasting >3 rounds where relationship matters

### When it fails
- Cultural stereotypes — adapt to the specific person, not the country
- Counterparty has lived/worked extensively outside their home culture (often hybrid)

## How these 5 frameworks interact

**Sequence in a typical multi-round negotiation:**

1. **Pre-negotiation:** Bazerman/Malhotra (BATNA expansion + bias mitigation) + Meyer (cultural diagnosis)
2. **Opening:** Fisher/Ury (anchor on principles) + Karrass/Shell (anchor on multi-axis leverage)
3. **Middle game:** Voss (tactical empathy + mirroring) + Karrass (decreasing-step concession discipline)
4. **Closing:** Fisher/Ury (focus on objective criteria) + Voss ("That's right" verification) + Bazerman (final BATNA recalc)
5. **Post-signature:** Meyer (cultural relationship-deepening)

## Reading priority (if you only have time for one book)

1. **First read:** *Never Split the Difference* (Voss) — most actionable, shortest, immediate gains
2. **Second read:** *Getting to Yes* (Fisher/Ury) — foundational concepts that everything builds on
3. **Third read:** *Bargaining for Advantage* (Shell) — leverage analysis and style flexibility
4. **Fourth read:** *Negotiation Genius* (Malhotra/Bazerman) — bias mitigation + advanced moves
5. **Fifth read:** *The Culture Map* (Meyer) — when you start crossing cultures regularly

Five books. ~1,500 pages total. Permanent ROI for any commercial founder dealing with multi-party deals.
