# Cultural Map — Negotiation Tactics by Counterparty Culture

Cultural code-switching for cross-border commercial negotiations. Each culture covered with: trust style, disagreement style, decision pace, agreement signals, face-saving rules, decision-making structure, and 3-5 specific tactical adaptations.

**Cultures covered (Tier 1 — full briefing):** Japanese, Korean, Chinese (HK/SG), Vietnamese, Turkish, German, American.

**Cultures NOT covered yet (Tier 2 — Hofstede fallback below):** Indian, Russian/Eastern European, Arab/Gulf, Dutch/Scandinavian, Brazilian/Latin, Israeli, French, British.

---

## Cultural diagnostic at a glance

| Culture | Trust | Disagreement | Pace | Agreement signal |
|---------|-------|--------------|------|------------------|
| **Japanese** | Relationship-first | Avoids confrontation | Slow-deliberate (nemawashi) | Verbal "yes" ≠ real yes; need formal sign-off |
| **Korean** | Mixed | Indirect (face-saving) | Measured, hierarchical | Senior person's nod = real yes |
| **Chinese (HK/SG)** | Relationship + pragmatic | Mixed | Pragmatic | Written contract + handshake |
| **Vietnamese** | Relationship | Avoids confrontation | Slow (collective consensus) | Multiple stakeholder confirmation |
| **Turkish** | Relationship | Direct + emotional | Variable (haggling-fast, decision-slow) | Verbal commitment + handshake reliable |
| **German** | Task-based | Direct | Measured, precise | Written agreement is the agreement |
| **American** | Task-based | Direct | Fast | Verbal + email confirms; handshake meaningful |

## 1. Japanese — Nemawashi & Honne/Tatemae

### Core dynamics

**Trust:** Built relationship-first via repeated meetings, shared meals, alcohol-bonded social events. Single transaction is less important than long-term reliability.

**Disagreement:** Avoided publicly. Direct contradiction causes loss of face for both parties. Soft language carries weight: "It might be difficult" usually means "no".

**Decision pace:** Slow. Decisions emerge from `nemawashi` (root-binding) — pre-meeting consensus building in 1:1 conversations. By the formal meeting, the decision is already made. Trying to decide IN the meeting is a faux pas.

**Decision structure:** Bottom-up via `ringi-sho` (proposal documents circulated for stamps from junior to senior). The most senior person typically signs last but agrees first.

### Tactical adaptations

**1. Nemawashi BEFORE the formal meeting.**
- Identify decision-influencers (not just decision-makers)
- Have individual conversations 1:1 with each
- Address concerns privately, off-record
- By formal meeting, alignment is already in place

**2. Recognize honne vs. tatemae.**
- "Yes" (`tatemae`, public face) often means "I hear you, will discuss internally"
- "It might be difficult" (`honne`, true feeling expressed softly) means "no"
- Probe gently for private feedback: "Anything that concerns you off-record?"

**3. Never disagree publicly.**
- Disagreement is for 1:1 conversations
- In formal meetings, validate first, suggest alternatives second
- If you must disagree publicly, frame as expansion: "Building on your point, I want to add..."

**4. Slow the pace, don't push.**
- Pushing for same-day decisions = disrespect for `ringi-sho` process
- Allow 1-3 weeks between proposal and decision
- Set explicit milestones, not just timelines

**5. Wa (harmony) preservation.**
- Group harmony > individual gain
- Don't single out individuals for praise or criticism
- Consensus-style language: "It seems we're aligned on..."

### Common blunders to avoid

- ❌ Asking for decision in the meeting
- ❌ Direct "no" to any proposal
- ❌ Public disagreement with a senior person
- ❌ Skipping social bonding (declining dinner invites, alcohol)
- ❌ Treating "yes" as binding without follow-up confirmation

*(none in user's current portfolio, but common for Japanese game publishers and Asian VC partners)*

## 2. Korean — Nunchi, Jeong, Kibun

### Core dynamics

**Trust:** Mixed (relationship + competence). Strong respect for credentials and track record AND for personal relationship over time. Chaebol (large conglomerate) culture is highly hierarchical.

**Disagreement:** Indirect. `Kibun` (pride/standing/mood) must be protected. Public contradiction damages it and the relationship. Disagreement happens 1:1 or via softening language.

**Decision pace:** Measured. Hierarchy-driven. Senior person's nod is the decision, but they signal "consensus emerging" rather than imposing.

**Decision structure:** Top-down with consensus signaling. The boss decides, but appears to do so based on the team's input. Junior staff must show deference; pretending hierarchy doesn't exist is offensive.

### Tactical adaptations

**1. Nunchi (situational awareness).**
- Korean counterparties read your nonverbals constantly
- Control your facial expressions, posture, tone
- Use deliberate pauses (signals thinking, not weakness)
- Be transparent about YOUR read: "I sense some concern from [person] — let's address it"

**2. Address senior person first.**
- Always look at, address, and direct questions to the senior person
- Even if junior staff are technical experts, route through senior
- If you bypass hierarchy, you've signaled disrespect

**3. Build jeong (emotional bond).**
- Shared meals, drinks, repeated small interactions
- Personal context in formal communications ("I remember you mentioned...")
- Public acknowledgment of their company's wins, market position
- Small gestures (gift from your country, attending their event)

**4. Protect kibun.**
- Never publicly contradict
- Frame disagreements as "interesting alternative perspective"
- If you must push back hard, do it 1:1 in private
- Public face-saving: "This is a tough ask for anyone, not just you"

**5. Use indirect language for disagreement.**
- "I wonder if we considered..."
- "Help me understand the reasoning..."
- "Perhaps there's a way to combine your approach with..."
- Never "No" or "That won't work"

**6. Recognize hierarchy stalls.**
- If decision stalls, junior contact is consulting up
- Don't push or interpret as bad faith
- Allow time, maintain warmth, periodically check status

### Common blunders to avoid

- ❌ Directly criticizing a senior person's idea
- ❌ Bypassing the senior to engage with juniors
- ❌ Being TOO casual or familiar with senior staff
- ❌ Bringing up controversial topics in public meetings
- ❌ Refusing alcohol at after-meeting dinners (significant cultural breach)
- ❌ Pushing for same-day decisions on important matters

### Counterparties of this type
**BagelCode (Korean game publisher).** Apply all of the above. Specifically: when negotiating equity/IP, frame disagreements as "mutual discovery" not "I convinced you" — let them save face.

## 3. Chinese (HK/SG) — Guanxi & Mianzi

### Core dynamics

**Trust:** Relationship-driven (`guanxi`) + pragmatic. Long-term obligation banking matters more than any single deal. HK is more transactional than mainland; Singapore is mixed.

**Disagreement:** `Mianzi` (face) preservation is critical but less extreme than Korea/Japan. Disagreement can happen in public if framed respectfully.

**Decision pace:** Pragmatic. HK is fast and efficient (common-law business culture). Mainland China can be slower with more bureaucratic layers.

**Decision structure:** Mixed. Senior person decides, but with strong consultation. HK is more individualist than mainland; can be top-down with single signatory.

### Tactical adaptations

**1. Guanxi (relationship as capital).**
- Frame deal as start of long-term relationship: "This is project 1. If it succeeds, project 2 follows."
- Build over time via repeated business + small social investments
- Reciprocate favors over years, not just within this deal
- Never treat counterparty as commodity supplier

**2. Mianzi (face management).**
- Disagree privately on big issues
- Validate in public: "Your approach has merit, and I want to add this angle"
- If you point out their error in public, the deal may collapse
- Help them save face when needed: "I understand you need to show your team you negotiated hard. Here's what we can publicly say..."

**3. Pragmatic structures preferred.**
- Detailed term sheets are expected and respected (HK/Singapore)
- Reference HKIAC or SIAC arbitration early — they're comfortable with it
- English legal terminology can be precise (they'll catch sloppiness)
- Bilingual contracts (Chinese-English parallel) need EXACT translation parity

**4. Guanxi can be weaponized.**
- "We have guanxi with your board" or "everyone in HK publishing knows you broke deals"
- Acknowledge but stay focused on substance: "Guanxi is important, AND we need terms to work"
- Don't let guanxi pressure override deal terms

**5. HK vs. mainland adaptation.**
- HK: more direct, faster, common-law literate, bilingual norm
- Mainland: more relationship-investment, slower, hierarchical
- Singapore: most pragmatic, English-first, common-law

### Common blunders to avoid

- ❌ Treating the deal as transactional (no relationship investment)
- ❌ Public criticism of any kind
- ❌ Sloppiness in bilingual contract translation
- ❌ Ignoring HKIAC/SIAC venue preference
- ❌ Bringing up Taiwan/Tibet/sensitive politics

### Counterparties of this type
**JM Game (HK Chinese gaming).** Apply HK-specific adaptations: precise English legal terms, HKIAC-comfortable, bilingual contract integrity. Less relationship-investment than mainland but more than US-direct.

## 4. Vietnamese — Indirect & Collective

### Core dynamics

**Trust:** Relationship-first. Built through repeated small interactions, shared meals, personal history. Single transactions are less important than the long-term relationship.

**Disagreement:** Highly avoided. "No" is rarely said directly. Indirect language carries the message.

**Decision pace:** Slow. Decisions emerge from collective consultation with `đại ca` (big brother figures), stakeholders, family-style alignment.

**Decision structure:** Hierarchical AND consensus-seeking. Leader decides but only after broad alignment. Single decision-maker is rare.

### Tactical adaptations

**1. Indirect "no" recognition.**
- "Maybe", "I will think about it", "That might be difficult", "We will consider it" → likely "no"
- Probe gently: "Help me understand what makes it difficult"
- Once you understand the real objection, you can address it
- Don't accept "yes" or silence as agreement — confirm in writing

**2. Allow time for consensus.**
- Never push for same-week decision on substantive terms
- Build in 1-3 weeks between proposal and decision
- If initial contact goes silent, they're consulting up — don't push
- Set explicit milestones: "By March 15, we need Section 5 finalized"

**3. Meet multiple stakeholders.**
- Don't negotiate only with biz dev person
- Request to meet CEO, ops, finance — relationship-investing across team
- The "one contact" pattern signals decision-making bottleneck

**4. Build relationship through small gestures.**
- Attending their conference
- Site visits
- Public partnership mentions
- Personal context in emails ("I remember you mentioned...")
- Shared meals with the team

**5. Phased deals work well.**
- "First 2 projects at 50% commission to prove fit, then 15% standard rate"
- Lets them report early success internally
- Reduces risk for both
- Vietnamese culture often prefers trial-period structures

**6. Slow pace = respect.**
- Rushing signals disrespect for their consultation process
- Patience reads as confidence and good faith
- Western "let's close this week" is offensive

### Common blunders to avoid

- ❌ Same-week decision pressure
- ❌ Negotiating only with one contact
- ❌ Treating "yes" or silence as agreement
- ❌ Direct "no" or harsh disagreement
- ❌ Skipping social bonding
- ❌ Public criticism

### Counterparties of this type
**PlayableLab (Vietnam-leaning).** Apply slow-pace + multiple-stakeholder + indirect language norms. Phased commission structures (first project 50%, subsequent 15%) align with their cultural preference for trial-then-scale.

## 5. Turkish — Honor, Pride, Bargaining as Virtue

### Core dynamics

**Trust:** Relationship-first. Personal bond carries deal weight. Ethics and honor are core — breaking a deal damages reputation widely.

**Disagreement:** Direct + emotional. Bargaining is expected, even sacred. Not negotiating (accepting first offer) signals disrespect for the counterparty's negotiating ability.

**Decision pace:** Variable. Haggling phase is fast (rapid back-and-forth). Decision phase can be slower (consultation with family/board/partners).

**Decision structure:** Often top-down (single signatory possible) but with internal consultation. Family-business dynamics common.

### Tactical adaptations

**1. Match haggling intensity.**
- Opening at 65% of target is NORMAL, not insulting
- Counter with equally assertive position; don't soft-pedal
- Each move feels earned through bargaining ritual
- Long negotiations (15-25 rounds) are normal, not stress signal

**2. Be direct.**
- Indirect language reads as evasion or weakness
- State your position clearly: "Our minimum is 15%."
- When disagreeing, name the disagreement: "I disagree with that approach because [X]"
- Vague language is suspicious

**3. Show authentic emotion.**
- Mild passion or even frustration signals you care
- Cold/robotic style reads as lack of conviction
- Don't suppress — channel it: "This term matters to us — here's why"
- BUT distinguish authentic emotion from theatrical anger (which they'll see through)

**4. Build personal relationship outside formal negotiation.**
- Coffee, dinners, learn about their other ventures
- Ask about their portfolio companies, industry network
- Show interest in Turkish culture/business specifically
- Personal bond accelerates closing

**5. Avoid artificial deadline pressure.**
- "Offer expires Friday" → backfires; they'll resent it and stall
- Real deadlines (board meeting, fiscal year) are fine — explain clearly
- Be honest about your timeline; they respect transparency
- If they impose tight deadlines, push back calmly

**6. Trade explicitly with named concessions.**
- "I'm moving here because X" — adds narrative weight
- They appreciate visible reciprocity
- Decreasing-step concessions read as discipline (good signal)

**7. 22-round negotiations are normal.**
- Don't interpret intensity as deal stress
- Ozgur's 22 comments on Alfa v3 = cultural pattern, not red flag
- Lean into the bargaining energy

### Common blunders to avoid

- ❌ Accepting the first offer (signals weakness or disrespect for them)
- ❌ Soft-pedaling — "we'd love to ask for X but understand if not" reads as already-conceded
- ❌ Cold or robotic tone — comes across as insincere
- ❌ Artificial deadlines
- ❌ Skipping personal relationship investment
- ❌ Interpreting aggressive positions as personal offense

### Counterparties of this type
**Alfa (Turkish gaming-investment bank).** Apply ALL Turkish norms. The 22-comment markup is normal Turkish negotiating energy. Match intensity in your reply, build personal relationship with Ozgur outside formal exchanges (coffee call, learn about their 4 portfolio companies), and don't interpret his aggressive guardrail-relaxation requests as bad faith — it's standard cultural haggling.

## 6. German — Direct, Precise, Documentable

### Core dynamics

**Trust:** Task-based. Trust is earned through demonstrated competence, reliability, and adherence to agreements. Personal warmth is appreciated but secondary.

**Disagreement:** Direct. Disagreement in public meetings is expected and not personal. Vague agreements are seen as bad faith.

**Decision pace:** Measured + precise. Decisions take time because every detail must be clear.

**Decision structure:** Hierarchical with strong consultation. Often consensus-required at senior levels.

### Tactical adaptations

**1. Precision first.**
- Detailed term sheets early in the process
- Specify everything: "Royalty is 20% of Net Game Income as defined in Appendix B, calculated monthly, due within 30 days of month-end"
- No ambiguity — every term must have a precise meaning
- Vagueness reads as incompetence or bad faith

**2. Facts over emotions.**
- Lead with research, comparables, data
- "Three comparable games in genre X show royalty 18-22%; we propose 20%"
- Emotional appeals backfire ("I really hope we can make this work" = weak)
- Argue from objective criteria

**3. Direct disagreement is acceptable.**
- "I disagree with that calculation" is normal, not rude
- Don't soft-pedal your position
- Public disagreement in meetings is expected
- Resolution comes through better arguments, not face-saving

**4. Written documentation is the agreement.**
- Verbal agreements have less weight
- Everything in writing, in detail
- Attachment-heavy contracts (Appendix A, B, C, D) preferred over loose language

**5. Time discipline matters.**
- Be on time. Hard.
- Respect their timelines and deliver on yours
- Late arrival = signal you can't be trusted operationally
- Decision pace is measured but deadlines are sacred once set

**6. Process orientation.**
- They like structured negotiations: agenda, defined topics, deliverables per session
- Show your process: "Here's how I'd like to structure today's discussion"
- Improvisation is less appreciated than methodical progress

### Common blunders to avoid

- ❌ Vague language ("approximately", "roughly", "soon")
- ❌ Skipping written documentation
- ❌ Lateness or schedule slippage
- ❌ Emotional appeals over data
- ❌ Trying to charm your way through an argument
- ❌ Verbal agreements without written follow-up

### Counterparties of this type
*(none in user's current portfolio, but common for European publishers and German-Swiss VCs)*

## 7. American — Direct, Task-First, Fast

### Core dynamics

**Trust:** Task-based primarily. Personal warmth is fast and surface-level — first-name basis within minutes. Trust is via track record + delivery.

**Disagreement:** Direct. Comfortable with confrontation in business settings. "Let's agree to disagree" or "I see it differently" are normal.

**Decision pace:** Fast. Multi-week decisions feel slow; same-week or same-day decisions are common for mid-size deals.

**Decision structure:** Often top-down (CEO/founder decides) with consultation. Decentralized for operational matters.

### Tactical adaptations

**1. Direct + concise.**
- Get to the point quickly
- Bullet-point summaries appreciated
- Long preambles are seen as wasted time
- "Here's the deal: [X]. What's your reaction?"

**2. Fast pace.**
- Same-week decision-making is normal
- Multi-week stalls signal lack of interest
- Match their pace; don't slow down deliberately
- "When can we close on this?" is a fair Round 2 question

**3. Outcome-focused.**
- "What's the outcome you want?" — frame conversations around results
- Process matters less than results
- Show track record + delivery capability early

**4. Comfortable with negotiation.**
- Direct push-back is fine
- "That's not going to work for us — here's why" is normal
- Don't apologize for disagreeing
- Standard negotiation tactics (anchoring, concession discipline) work as expected

**5. Light social bonding, surface-level warmth.**
- First-name basis fast
- Personal anecdotes welcome but brief
- Doesn't require deep investment in social bonding
- BUT genuine relationships still matter for repeat deals

**6. Written confirmation expected.**
- Verbal agreement followed by email recap = standard
- Term sheets standard
- Less detail-fanaticism than Germans, but still written

### Common blunders to avoid

- ❌ Long preambles, slow pace
- ❌ Excessive deference or formality (reads as weak)
- ❌ Failing to follow up in writing
- ❌ Treating American casualness as binding without confirmation

### Counterparties of this type
American startups, US strategic acquirers, US-based VCs. Standard direct-Western negotiation playbook applies.

## Cross-cultural negotiation principles

### Universal rules (apply regardless of culture)

1. **Match formality level.** Don't be more formal than them (cold) or less formal than them (disrespectful).
2. **Mirror pace.** Their natural rhythm reveals their decision process. Forcing your pace damages trust.
3. **Public vs private rule.** ALL cultures handle disagreement better in private. Default to private for tough conversations.
4. **Acknowledge difference.** "I know cultures handle this differently — what would feel most natural for you?" earns respect.
5. **Personal investment.** Even task-cultures appreciate genuine interest in the person, not just the deal.

### Diagnostic questions (when uncertain)

- "How does your team typically make decisions on something like this?" → reveals top-down vs consensus
- "What's your usual timeline for this kind of deal?" → reveals pace expectations
- "Do you prefer to work through details in writing or in conversation?" → reveals high-context vs low-context preference
- "Are there sensitivities I should be aware of when discussing this with your team?" → reveals face-saving needs

These questions are cheap, fast, and often reveal more than weeks of research.

## Cultural overlay on the 5 phases

### Phase 1 (Pre-negotiation)
- Always include cultural brief in pre-negotiation prep
- Korea/Japan/Vietnam: build in 1-2 weeks of relationship-investment time before formal negotiation
- Germany/US/HK: prepare detailed term sheet upfront

### Phase 2 (Opening)
- Korea/Japan/Vietnam: indirect language, deference signals, relationship-first
- Turkey/Israel: direct + emotional, expect haggling
- Germany/US: precise written opening with data

### Phase 3 (Middle game)
- Korea/Japan: 1:1 conversations between formal rounds (nemawashi-style)
- Turkey: aggressive bargaining is expected; match intensity
- Vietnam: allow consultation time between rounds; don't push for fast turns

### Phase 4 (Closing)
- Korea/Japan: senior person's nod is the close signal
- Vietnam: written confirmation from multiple stakeholders
- Turkey: handshake reliable; verbal commitment binding
- Germany/US: signed term sheet is the close

### Phase 5 (Post-signature)
- Relationship-cultures: invest more in social bonding
- Task-cultures: deliver on milestones; relationship follows performance
